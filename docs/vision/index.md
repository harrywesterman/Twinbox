# Vision

## About me

Hello! I'm Harry Westerman, 55 years old, from the Netherlands. I live here with my lovely wife and our three daughters. The oldest two are twins, which is where the name Twinbox comes from.

Outside IT I like brewing beer at [bierineenweek.nl](https://bierineenweek.nl) :beers: and digging into my family history at [stamboomwesterman.net](https://stamboomwesterman.net). Inside IT, I have spent about 25 years working in Dutch banking and government, always on the infrastructure side of things: workstations, servers, networks, datacenters, and the big enterprise platforms from the well-known names in IT.

That background is still part of how I look at technology. I like systems that are solid, understandable, and repairable. But I also like small machines on a desk, blinking lights, and the feeling that you can still build something useful yourself.

## Where it started

My fascination with computers started when I was 12 and my uncle gave me a Sinclair ZX81, the kind of home computer you connected to the family TV. That little machine made computers feel less like something far away in an office and more like something you could explore with your own hands.

In my teens I ran a wonderful Amiga bulletin board called Epsilon BBS, connected to FidoNET. Together with a couple of friends I also worked on April, a mail point system for Amiga computers. Looking back, that was probably my first taste of the same thing that still interests me now: computers talking to each other, people building things together, and networks that do not have to be mysterious to be powerful.

I have always tinkered with a so-called [homelab](https://www.reddit.com/r/homelab/). I tried many Linux distributions, played with loads of open source projects, automated my house with the excellent [Home Assistant](https://www.home-assistant.io/), and ran all kinds of things in Docker and Kubernetes.

## The coffee table cluster

In 2016 I built a small Raspberry Pi cluster. It ran Kubernetes, installed with Ansible from my laptop. The cluster was connected to Wi-Fi, powered by a power bank, and could run on our coffee table in the living room:

![Raspberry Pi k3s cluster on the coffee table](../assets/vision/picluster.webp)

That cluster was not the fastest thing in the world, of course. But that was not the point. It made Kubernetes tangible. It turned something that usually lives in datacenters and cloud platforms into something you could point at while drinking coffee.

That feeling matters to me. Technology becomes less intimidating when you can touch it, break it, fix it, and understand what is happening.

## Why Twinbox

I always wondered why powerful, private infrastructure at home still has to be so difficult.

There are excellent open source alternatives for many of the tools we use every day: file storage, photos, chat, notes, dashboards, automation, and much more. But running them well is still a lot of work. You need servers, networking, certificates, backups, updates, monitoring, identity, secrets, storage, and a way to recover when something goes wrong.

For people who enjoy infrastructure, that can be a fun weekend. For everyone else, it quickly becomes the reason they give up and keep sending their data to the usual big platforms.

Twinbox is my attempt to make that gap smaller. It brings together the things I like and trust: Proxmox, Talos Linux, Kubernetes, GitOps, open source applications, and a guided setup that tries to make the hard parts understandable.

## What I want Twinbox to become

I want Twinbox to be a home platform that feels serious without becoming corporate. Something you can run on modest hardware, learn from, and still rely on for real services.

It should help people host more of their digital life themselves without pretending that infrastructure is magic. The goal is not to hide every detail. The goal is to make the details approachable, automate the boring and fragile parts, and leave enough visible that you can learn what your own system is doing.

That is the kind of project I like best: practical, a little stubborn, built from real experience, and still close enough to the coffee table to feel personal.
